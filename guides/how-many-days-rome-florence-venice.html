How Many Days in Rome, Florence and Venice? The Honest Split

PHBy The Preplan Holidays planning team · Published September 19, 2026 · 6 min read

We book Rome, Florence and Venice every week, and the question we get most is how to split the nights. The short answer: three in Rome, two in Florence, two in Venice, in that order or the reverse. This guide explains what you actually get in each city on that split, what a fourth night buys you, and when Milan belongs in the plan.

Rome: three nights minimum

Rome has two separate must-do days. Ancient Rome (Colosseum, Forum, Palatine) is a morning and early afternoon; the Vatican (museums, Sistine Chapel, St Peter's) is another. Neither can be rushed and neither should be combined with the other. Three nights gives you two full days for those plus an arrival evening for the Trevi Fountain and Pantheon, and a departure morning for coffee. A fourth night adds the Borghese Gallery, Trastevere in daylight and the Appian Way, and it is the first extra night we would add to any trip.

Florence: two nights

Florence is compact. The Duomo, Uffizi, Piazza della Signoria, Ponte Vecchio and Piazzale Michelangelo fit one full day with a reserved Uffizi slot, and the arrival and departure evenings cover dinner in the Oltrarno and a morning at the San Lorenzo market. A third night is for the Accademia and a day trip: Siena is 90 minutes by bus, Pisa an hour by train, Lucca an hour and a half. If you have a Tuscan countryside itch, scratch it here rather than in Rome.

Venice: two nights

Two nights gives you one full day and two evenings, which is exactly when Venice is best: after the day-trippers leave. The full day covers St Mark's Basilica at opening, the Doge's Palace, the vaporetto down the Grand Canal and the Rialto. A third night is for Murano and Burano, or simply for getting lost in Cannaregio and Castello. Fewer than two nights and you are a day-tripper paying the access fee for the privilege of seeing the square at its busiest.

When to add Milan

Add Milan if you are flying into Malpensa (the cheapest US flights often do), if you want the Last Supper, or if Lake Como is on the list. Two nights is enough. Skip it if you have seven nights or fewer; the three classic cities will use every one. Our grand Italian four runs nine nights Milan to Rome and is the version we recommend for anyone with the time.

Which order?

Geography decides. The three cities sit on one rail line: Rome in the south, Florence in the middle, Venice in the north. Fly into Rome and out of Venice, or the reverse, and you never double back; the same open-jaw ticket usually costs no more than a return. If you must fly in and out of the same airport, make it Rome and do Venice in the middle: Rome to Venice is 3 hours 40 minutes by train, Venice to Florence 2 hours, Florence back to Rome an hour and twenty minutes. Most people prefer to finish in Venice; it is the harder city to leave.

Three mistakes we see

  • One night in Venice. You arrive at 3pm, see the square in the crowds, and leave at 10am. Give it two.
  • Day-tripping Florence from Rome. It is doable by train, but you spend three hours travelling to see the city in its busiest window and miss the Oltrarno at dusk.
  • Five cities in seven nights. Every extra city costs half a day in packing, trains and check-in. Three cities in a week is the sweet spot.
